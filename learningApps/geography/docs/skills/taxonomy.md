# Geography — Skills Taxonomy

Date: 2026-10-29

A subject-wide, audience-independent catalog of geographical competencies. It covers orientation, cartography and geospatial evidence, land and water systems, weather and climate, natural regions, human geography, environment–society interactions, and geographic investigation. Domain → Group → Skill denotes **classification**, not grades, lesson order, prerequisites, assessment evidence, or a particular national curriculum. The same competency may support introductory explorations and more demanding learning experiences.

**Boundaries:** Geographic interpretation of the spatial distribution, characteristics, and interaction of Earth systems is in scope. Detailed biology of organisms, physical laws, chemical reactions, astronomy, software implementation, historical chronology, and financial calculations remain primarily in their respective subjects. Geography can draw on those disciplines without duplicating their taxonomies. The subject's optional “What is it?” encyclopedia is a **lesson/content format**, not a Skill or SkillGroup.

**Breadth references:** [NCGE National Geography Standards — six essential elements](https://ncge.org/teacher-resources/national-geography-standards/) and [National Geographic, Geography Standard 1](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/geography-standard-1/), consulted as international scope checks; they are **not** adopted as a mandated national or grade-specific curriculum. ## DOMAINS

### Spatial Orientation

Groups: 3 · Skills: 9

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Locating a phenomenon requires a reference frame: position can be described relative to other places, by direction and distance, or by recurring arrangements. This domain concerns reasoning about where things are, rather than interpreting the graphic conventions of a map.

#### Location and Reference

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

A place can be specified using nearby landmarks, named areas, or an explicit spatial reference. The important distinction is between a location itself and the descriptions that allow another person to find it.

##### Describe a place relative to landmarks

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use a stable, recognizable reference such as a road, river, or building to communicate where a place lies; a description must distinguish it from nearby alternatives.

##### Distinguish relative and absolute location

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize that “beside the school” depends on a reference place, whereas a coordinate or fixed address identifies a position in an agreed reference system.

##### Identify location at local, regional, and global scales

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Relate the same place to nested areas such as a neighborhood, country, and world region without confusing a change of scale with physical movement.

#### Directions and Routes

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Moving between places relies on orientation, directional instructions, and sequences of turns. The focus is spatial navigation, not the mathematical calculation of distance.

##### Use cardinal directions to orient a route

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Associate north, east, south, and west with actual movement or a stated orientation, even when the observer faces a different way.

##### Use intermediate directions to distinguish bearings

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Locate positions such as northeast between adjacent cardinal directions rather than treating the compass as only four separate choices.

##### Construct a route through ordered waypoints

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Choose an intelligible sequence of landmarks and turns between two positions, checking that the instructions preserve connectivity.

#### Spatial Relationships

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Places and features form patterns of nearness, containment, connection, concentration, and separation. These relationships matter even when the exact names of the locations change.

##### Distinguish adjacency from proximity

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Two territories may share a boundary without being close to the same external landmark; nearby places need not touch.

##### Describe clustered and dispersed arrangements

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Compare objects gathered in a small area with objects spread across a larger one while considering the observation scale.

##### Identify barriers and corridors between places

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Explain how a mountain range, river crossing, valley, or road can restrict or enable movement between otherwise nearby locations.

### Maps and Geospatial Representations

Groups: 4 · Skills: 12

Code: [Not implemented](#)

A representation selects, simplifies, and positions geographic information to communicate spatial meaning. Reading it requires attention to symbols, scale, perspective, coordinates, and the limitations of the chosen portrayal.

#### Map Conventions

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Maps communicate through agreed visual devices rather than reproducing the ground exactly. Titles, legends, orientation, and scale provide distinct information necessary for interpretation.

##### Interpret conventional map symbols through a legend

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Connect a symbol or color to its documented meaning instead of assuming that its appearance is self-explanatory.

##### Determine map orientation from its north indicator

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use the map's stated orientation rather than assuming the top of every depiction is geographic north.

##### Interpret a map scale as a distance relationship

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize that a short mark on paper or screen represents a larger distance on the ground and that the ratio changes with scale.

#### Globes, Maps, and Projection

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

A spherical Earth cannot be shown on a flat sheet without some distortion, and different representations support different questions. Choosing one is a reasoning decision rather than a display preference.

##### Distinguish a globe from a flat map as Earth models

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Explain why a globe represents the overall shape of Earth more directly while a flat map is easier to use for selected areas or purposes.

##### Recognize projection distortion in area or shape

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Identify that apparent sizes and shapes can change when the curved surface is flattened, especially when comparing distant latitudes.

##### Select a geographic representation for a purpose

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Choose between a street plan, regional map, world map, or globe according to the information needed, not solely its visual detail.

#### Geographic Coordinates

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

The graticule provides a worldwide angular reference for positions. Latitude and longitude are distinct measurements whose ordered combination identifies a location.

##### Interpret latitude as position north or south of the Equator

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize the Equator as zero latitude and compare how far a point lies toward either pole.

##### Interpret longitude as position east or west of the prime meridian

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use the reference meridian and longitude values to locate east–west positions without confusing them with latitude.

##### Locate a point from a latitude–longitude pair

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use both coordinates in their proper order and hemispheres to identify a geographic position.

#### Thematic Maps and Layers

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

A thematic representation displays the geographic variation of one or more properties, such as rainfall, population density, or land use. Interpretation depends on the variable and classification, not merely color intensity.

##### Distinguish reference maps from thematic maps

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize whether a map primarily identifies places and features or displays the spatial variation of a selected measured characteristic.

##### Interpret a thematic legend and its value classes

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Relate mapped color ranges, symbol sizes, or categories to the underlying variable while respecting units and class boundaries.

##### Compare spatial patterns across two map layers

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Identify where mapped phenomena coincide or differ without assuming that co-location alone establishes causation.

### Earth Surface and Land Processes

Groups: 3 · Skills: 9

Code: [Not implemented](#)

The solid surface of Earth contains distinctive relief, materials, and changing landforms shaped by internal and external processes. Geographic understanding emphasizes their visible characteristics and spatial patterns; detailed chemical and physical mechanisms remain in other sciences.

#### Landforms and Relief

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Elevation, slope, and shape distinguish hills, mountains, valleys, plains, and plateaus. A named feature is less important than the properties by which such terrain can be recognized.

##### Classify common landforms by observable characteristics

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Distinguish a valley, plain, plateau, and mountain using relief and shape instead of relying solely on place names.

##### Compare elevations and relative relief

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Separate height above sea level from height difference between nearby points, which can vary independently.

##### Interpret contour spacing as terrain steepness

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Read closely spaced elevation contours as a steeper surface than widely spaced contours under the same interval.

#### Rocks, Soil, and Surface Materials

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Earth materials differ in texture, origin, and the ways they affect drainage and land use. This group covers their geographic occurrence and observable properties without becoming a mineral identification course.

##### Distinguish exposed rock from developed soil

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize that soil commonly contains weathered mineral particles, organic matter, water, and air, whereas exposed bedrock is not a soil profile.

##### Compare observable properties of surface materials

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use features such as grain size, cohesion, and permeability to explain why sand, clay, and gravel behave differently at the ground surface.

##### Infer basic land-use constraints from soil conditions

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Relate waterlogging, shallow soils, or steep slopes to limitations on cultivation or construction without predicting engineering safety from appearance alone.

#### Landscape Change

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Landforms are altered by erosion, movement and deposition of material, and tectonic activity. The objective is to explain recognizable geographic results and timescales without reducing every landscape to one cause.

##### Distinguish weathering, erosion, and deposition

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Identify whether material is being broken down in place, transported away, or accumulated in a new location.

##### Explain how flowing water reshapes a landscape

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Relate river transport and sediment deposition to features such as valleys, meanders, and deltas while recognizing that conditions vary.

##### Recognize landforms associated with tectonic activity

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Link broad patterns of mountains, volcanic regions, and earthquake zones to plate boundaries without treating every mountain as volcanic.

### Water Systems

Groups: 3 · Skills: 9

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Water connects landscapes through drainage networks, oceans, atmosphere, and underground storage. Geographic questions center on where water moves and accumulates, how its distribution varies, and how water features shape places.

#### Rivers and Inland Waters

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Rivers, lakes, wetlands, and their catchments form connected systems whose boundaries are not always visible at the water's edge. A watershed describes where surface runoff converges.

##### Identify the main parts of a river network

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Distinguish source, tributary, confluence, channel, and mouth by their roles in connected flow.

##### Delineate a drainage basin from terrain

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Infer the land area contributing runoff to an outlet using divides and slopes rather than administrative borders.

##### Distinguish lakes, rivers, and wetlands by water behavior

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize characteristic movement and retention of water without treating all standing or shallow water as the same feature.

#### Oceans, Seas, and Coasts

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Coastal and marine environments differ in exposure, enclosure, water movement, and relation to land. The emphasis is on geographic patterns and coastal form rather than detailed fluid dynamics.

##### Distinguish ocean, sea, gulf, and bay in geographic context

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use scale, enclosure, and connection to larger water bodies while acknowledging that some named features are historical conventions.

##### Identify coastal erosion and deposition features

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Relate cliffs, beaches, spits, or deltas to material removal and accumulation in their coastal setting.

##### Recognize geographic patterns of tides and currents

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Explain that sea-level variation and major current pathways are spatially uneven and influence navigation, coasts, and climate.

#### Hydrologic Connections

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Water changes location and state among the atmosphere, surface, living systems, and subsurface. This is a connected circulation model rather than a single linear loop.

##### Trace water through evaporation, condensation, and precipitation

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Follow a coherent pathway through different reservoirs without assuming that each droplet necessarily passes through every stage in one fixed order.

##### Distinguish surface runoff, infiltration, and groundwater storage

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize different destinations for precipitation and how soil and rock conditions affect them.

##### Compare geographic water availability across regions

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Relate precipitation, storage, seasonal variation, and demand to differences in accessible fresh water, rather than equating wet weather with secure supply.

### Atmosphere, Weather, and Climate

Groups: 3 · Skills: 9

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Atmospheric conditions vary over hours, seasons, and decades and differ among places. Geographic interpretation links observations and regional patterns while distinguishing climate description from a single weather event.

#### Weather Observations

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Weather is described by measurable conditions at a particular location and time. Observations must be interpreted with appropriate units and temporal context.

##### Identify weather variables in an observation

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Separate air temperature, precipitation, wind, cloud cover, and pressure as different properties rather than a single “good or bad weather” rating.

##### Read temperature and precipitation observations

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Interpret a basic station table or chart with units and dates, separating amount of rain from air temperature.

##### Interpret standard weather symbols on a map

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use an explicit legend to identify rain, cloud, or wind indicators without relying on color alone.

#### Climate Patterns

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Climate summarizes the long-term distribution and variability of weather, not simply a typical day. Latitude, elevation, land–sea contrast, and circulation contribute to regional differences.

##### Distinguish climate from a short-term weather event

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Explain why an unusually cold day does not by itself define a region's climate or overturn a long-term trend.

##### Interpret a basic climate diagram

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Compare monthly temperature and precipitation patterns to identify seasonality without treating averages as daily forecasts.

##### Explain broad climate differences using geographic factors

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use latitude, elevation, coastal influence, and prevailing wind patterns to account for contrasts between places, while allowing multiple causes.

#### Atmospheric Hazards

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Drought, storms, extreme temperatures, and heavy precipitation become hazards through the interaction of physical exposure and human vulnerability. Geographic representation helps identify where impacts are likely.

##### Recognize areas exposed to weather-related hazards

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Match a described event such as a flood-producing storm or drought with plausible geographic exposure conditions.

##### Interpret a weather warning area on a map

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Identify affected places and the warning's time scope without mistaking a forecast zone for an exact prediction at every point.

##### Compare ways to reduce weather-hazard risk

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Evaluate options such as early warning, shelter, land-use planning, or water storage for the specific hazard and local context.

### Natural Regions and Biogeography

Groups: 3 · Skills: 9

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Natural environments form recognizable regional patterns where climate, water, land, and organisms interact. Geography examines how these features are distributed and connected; organism anatomy and ecological mechanisms remain primarily in Biology.

#### Biomes and Habitats

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Broad vegetation and habitat patterns reflect environmental conditions while local ecosystems can be more varied than a single biome label suggests.

##### Identify major terrestrial biome patterns

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Associate broad forest, grassland, desert, and tundra patterns with environmental conditions rather than treating them as isolated species lists.

##### Compare habitat conditions between geographic locations

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use temperature, moisture, altitude, and land cover to explain contrasts in living conditions for organisms.

##### Recognize transition zones between biomes

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Identify that vegetation and environmental conditions may change gradually across boundaries rather than at a perfectly sharp line.

#### Regional Classification

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Regions are analytical groupings chosen for a purpose, not always natural units with fixed edges. A region can be defined by physical or human characteristics.

##### Classify places into regions using stated criteria

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Apply an explicit property such as climate or relief consistently instead of sorting places by familiar names alone.

##### Distinguish formal and functional regions

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Separate areas sharing a measurable characteristic from areas connected through activity around a center, such as a commuting region.

##### Explain why regional boundaries may differ between maps

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize that changing the defining variable or classification threshold can redraw a region without moving any physical feature.

#### Environmental Gradients

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Natural conditions often vary systematically with elevation, latitude, and proximity to water. Such gradients help explain patterns without implying that every place at the same latitude is identical.

##### Explain broad environmental variation with latitude

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Connect differences in solar input and circulation to large-scale environmental belts while allowing local exceptions.

##### Recognize altitudinal changes in natural conditions

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Describe how cooler temperatures and vegetation zones can occur higher on mountains without assuming identical patterns everywhere.

##### Identify environmental shifts from comparative geographic evidence

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use two dated distributions or observations to describe a change in habitat range, without automatically assigning a single cause.

### Human Geography

Groups: 3 · Skills: 9

Code: [Not implemented](#)

People organize settlements, territories, cultures, economies, and connections across space. The geographic focus is distribution, movement, accessibility, and the interaction of people with places—not general history or economics taught without spatial context.

#### Population and Settlement

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Population counts, density, migration, and settlement forms show how human presence is unevenly distributed. The same total population can occupy very different spatial patterns.

##### Interpret a population-density map

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Distinguish people per unit area from the total number of residents and identify areas with different densities.

##### Compare concentrated and dispersed settlement patterns

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize clustered towns, linear settlements, and scattered homes and relate their locations to plausible geographic conditions.

##### Describe migration as movement between places

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Identify origin, destination, and movement direction while distinguishing a migration flow from a static population count.

#### Territories and Cultural Landscapes

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Political boundaries and culturally meaningful places are human spatial constructions that may overlap imperfectly with physical regions. Geographic names and boundaries can be contested or change over time.

##### Distinguish physical features from political boundaries

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize that a river or mountain is a land feature, while a state boundary is a social and legal line that may follow such a feature.

##### Interpret administrative territories at different levels

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Relate a municipality or region to a country without equating nested administrative units with continents or biomes.

##### Recognize human influences on a cultural landscape

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Identify visible patterns such as farmland, places of worship, or historic streets without assuming all communities share one culture.

#### Economic Activity and Connectivity

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Work, goods, transport, and services have locations and links. The geographic question is why activities and routes concentrate where they do, not how to calculate prices or run a business.

##### Identify land-use patterns in a settlement or region

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Distinguish residential, agricultural, industrial, and service areas by their spatial functions rather than building appearance alone.

##### Trace movement of goods or people through a network

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Follow origin, intermediate links, and destination while recognizing the constraints of routes and transfer points.

##### Explain basic geographic factors affecting activity location

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Relate transport access, resources, population, and environmental constraints to plausible site choices without claiming one universal optimal location.

### Environment, Resources, and Geographic Inquiry

Groups: 3 · Skills: 9

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Geography also asks how evidence about places can support responsible choices. This domain combines investigation, resource use, impacts, and decision-making but retains separate Skills for observing patterns, assessing evidence, and judging alternatives.

#### Resources and Human Impact

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Resource availability and land use depend on local physical conditions and human choices. Effects may cross administrative boundaries and emerge over different timescales.

##### Distinguish renewable and nonrenewable resource stocks

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize that renewal rates and extraction rates matter, so a theoretically renewable resource may still be depleted locally.

##### Identify geographic consequences of land-use change

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Explain plausible effects of paving, deforestation, or irrigation on runoff, habitat, or local conditions.

##### Trace an environmental impact beyond its source

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Recognize that air, water, transport, or shared ecosystems can carry effects into downstream or neighboring places.

#### Geographic Evidence and Investigation

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Geographic claims are supported by observations located in space and time. Evidence may come from fieldwork, maps, images, measurements, or datasets and must be evaluated for limitations.

##### Formulate a question about a spatial pattern

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Ask a tractable question involving location, distribution, proximity, or change rather than a factual place-name quiz.

##### Collect and record geographically referenced observations

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Associate measurements or descriptions with sufficient place and time context to make comparisons meaningful.

##### Assess limitations of geographic data sources

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Check coverage, age, resolution, classification, and possible bias before drawing conclusions from a map or dataset.

#### Geographic Decisions and Communication

Skills: 3

Code: [Not implemented](#)

A geographic recommendation combines evidence about places with competing objectives and uncertainty. Maps can communicate reasoning, but they do not settle value judgments on their own.

##### Compare locations against stated geographic criteria

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Evaluate alternatives using relevant properties such as accessibility, hazard exposure, and resource availability without silently changing the criteria.

##### Explain trade-offs of a proposed spatial decision

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Show how a new road, park, settlement, or conservation area might benefit some needs while creating other costs.

##### Communicate a geographic conclusion with evidence

Code: [Not implemented](#)

Use an appropriately labeled map, comparison, or spatial description to support a claim and state material limitations.
