# Philosophy — Skills Taxonomy

Date: 2026-10-29

A subject-wide, audience-independent catalog of independently assessable philosophical competencies. The taxonomy covers inquiry and dialogue; logic and argumentation; epistemology and critical evaluation of information; philosophy of science and general research methodology; metaphysics; mind and personhood; ethics; social and political philosophy; philosophy of language; aesthetics; and philosophical traditions. Classification is not a lesson order, curriculum, assessment plan, age band, or claim that any learner must master the entire catalog.

**Boundaries:** Psychology investigates the empirical mechanisms of cognition, emotion, motivation, persuasion, and behavioral bias; Philosophy examines the validity of arguments, justification of beliefs, fundamental concepts, and normative questions. Scientific disciplines own their subject-specific observations and explanations, while Philosophy of Science examines general evidence, inference, study design, and scientific justification. History studies events and societies; Philosophy studies historical thinkers' actual arguments. Language and Literature owns grammar and composition; Art owns making and analyzing artistic works as practice; Philosophy examines meaning and the grounds of aesthetic evaluation. Media literacy here means epistemic appraisal of public claims, not marketing strategy or a psychology of influence. Cross-textbook overlap does not imply shared Skill IDs.

**Evidence used for breadth checks:** [American Philosophical Association — Philosophy: A brief guide](https://www.apaonline.org/page/undergraduates), [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Table of Contents](https://plato.stanford.edu/contents.html), and [Studio Subject Proposition](../../../../studio/Subject%20Proposition.md). These guide subject coverage without imposing one national syllabus, one philosophical school, or a grade-based mastery ceiling. Philosophical positions are presented as examinable arguments, not institutional doctrine. This first edition is a proposal pending explicit editorial approval.

## DOMAINS

### Philosophical Inquiry

Groups: 3 · Skills: 12

[Not implemented](#)

Philosophy investigates conceptual, normative, and foundational questions whose answers require reasons rather than observation alone. This domain concerns the practices used to formulate, refine, and discuss such questions; it is distinct from the formal evaluation of arguments in Logic.

#### Questions and Problems

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

A productive philosophical problem can be examined through competing answers, explicit concepts, and reasons. The object is to make a question discussable without assuming that every question has a single empirical test.

##### Distinguish philosophical questions from empirical questions

[Not implemented](#)

Identify when an issue asks what a concept means, what ought to be done, or what ultimately explains something, rather than only requesting a measurable fact; many questions have both aspects.

##### Formulate a philosophical question

[Not implemented](#)

Turn an ordinary puzzlement into an open question about meaning, reasons, values, or assumptions that admits more than a yes-or-no preference.

##### Identify assumptions in a philosophical problem

[Not implemented](#)

Expose propositions that a question or proposed answer takes for granted, such as what counts as a person or what makes an action fair.

##### Distinguish a philosophical problem from an example

[Not implemented](#)

Separate the general issue, such as whether promises create obligations, from the particular story used to make it vivid.

#### Conceptual Analysis

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Careful discussion depends on tracking the boundaries of concepts, especially when ordinary words cover multiple cases. These abilities refine meanings rather than merely memorize technical vocabulary.

##### Clarify a contested concept

[Not implemented](#)

Specify relevant conditions or uses of a term such as freedom so interlocutors can determine what their disagreement concerns.

##### Distinguish necessary from sufficient conditions

[Not implemented](#)

Determine whether a proposed property is required for a concept to apply, enough for it to apply, or both; a condition can fail one test and pass the other.

##### Test a definition with a counterexample

[Not implemented](#)

Find a case included or excluded incorrectly by a proposed definition and explain what the case reveals about its limits.

##### Use a thought experiment to examine a concept

[Not implemented](#)

Vary features of an imagined situation to isolate a philosophical principle; make clear which assumptions are stipulated rather than observed.

#### Dialogue and Disagreement

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Reasoned dialogue seeks understanding and justified revision, not simply winning assent. Participants must fairly represent alternatives and keep interpersonal respect separate from agreement.

##### Reconstruct another person's position fairly

[Not implemented](#)

Restate a view in terms its advocate could recognize, distinguishing the claim from a weaker caricature.

##### Ask a probing philosophical question

[Not implemented](#)

Request a reason, distinction, implication, or counterexample that can advance inquiry without demanding personal disclosure.

##### Respond to a philosophical objection

[Not implemented](#)

Address the actual challenge to a claim, rather than diverting to the objector's identity or motives.

##### Revise a position in light of reasons

[Not implemented](#)

Identify which premise or conclusion should change when a cogent objection defeats an earlier commitment.

### Logic and Argumentation

Groups: 4 · Skills: 20

[Not implemented](#)

The central question is whether reasons support a conclusion and in what way. Formal entailment, defeasible inference, and argumentative defects are distinguished from psychological causes of belief, which belong chiefly to Psychology.

#### Arguments and Inference

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Arguments connect propositions through an inferential claim; assertions, explanations, and rhetorical appeals may occur nearby without playing the same role.

##### Identify premises in an argument

[Not implemented](#)

Locate the reasons offered in support of a conclusion, including unstated propositions when context makes them indispensable.

##### Identify an argument's conclusion

[Not implemented](#)

Determine which proposition is supposed to follow or gain support, regardless of its position in a passage.

##### Distinguish an argument from an explanation

[Not implemented](#)

Tell reasoning intended to establish a claim from an account of why an already accepted event or fact occurred.

##### Reconstruct an argument in standard form

[Not implemented](#)

Arrange its relevant premises and conclusion explicitly while preserving the original reasoning rather than improving it silently.

#### Deductive Reasoning

Skills: 6

[Not implemented](#)

Deduction concerns whether it is possible for premises to be true while a conclusion is false. Logical form is evaluated separately from whether the premises describe the world correctly.

##### Assess deductive validity

[Not implemented](#)

Determine whether a conclusion must hold if the premises hold, using logical structure rather than agreement with the conclusion.

##### Distinguish validity from soundness

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize that a sound deductive argument requires both a valid inference and true premises.

##### Apply modus ponens

[Not implemented](#)

Infer a consequent from a conditional and its antecedent without treating the inverse conditional as equivalent.

##### Apply modus tollens

[Not implemented](#)

Reject the antecedent of a conditional from the denial of its consequent, under the conditional as stated.

##### Evaluate a categorical syllogism

[Not implemented](#)

Examine whether relations among classes support the proposed conclusion, rather than relying on familiar subject matter.

##### Identify a contradiction in a set of claims

[Not implemented](#)

Show where commitments cannot all be true together under the same meanings and conditions.

#### Non-deductive Reasoning

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Many reasonable arguments support conclusions without guaranteeing them. Their strength depends on evidence, alternatives, sampling, or explanatory power rather than formal certainty.

##### Evaluate an inductive generalization

[Not implemented](#)

Judge how well observed cases support a claim about a broader population, including the relevance of selection and variation.

##### Evaluate analogical reasoning

[Not implemented](#)

Check whether compared cases share features relevant to the proposed conclusion rather than merely superficial similarities.

##### Evaluate an inference to the best explanation

[Not implemented](#)

Compare hypotheses by how well they explain evidence, their assumptions, and their fit with alternative explanations.

##### Distinguish deductive certainty from probabilistic support

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize that a well-supported conclusion can remain revisable when the reasoning is non-deductive.

#### Argumentative Fallacies

Skills: 6

[Not implemented](#)

A flaw concerns the relation between claims and reasons, not necessarily the speaker's motives or a named cognitive bias. Context matters: surface wording alone rarely proves fallaciousness.

##### Detect an ad hominem fallacy

[Not implemented](#)

Identify when a personal attack substitutes for addressing reasons, while distinguishing relevant credibility evidence from irrelevant insults.

##### Detect a straw-man argument

[Not implemented](#)

Show where a position is distorted into an easier target and recover the actual view before evaluating it.

##### Detect a false dilemma

[Not implemented](#)

Identify a purported exhaustive choice that overlooks meaningful alternatives or combined possibilities.

##### Detect circular reasoning

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize when the offered support presupposes the very claim at issue without independent justification.

##### Detect an equivocation fallacy

[Not implemented](#)

Trace a shift between meanings of the same expression that makes an inference appear stronger than it is.

##### Evaluate an appeal to authority

[Not implemented](#)

Examine whether expertise is relevant and reliable evidence in context, rather than assuming that invoking any authority proves a conclusion.

### Epistemology and Critical Evaluation

Groups: 4 · Skills: 17

[Not implemented](#)

Questions about knowledge concern what can be believed responsibly, what makes claims true, and when confidence should change. This domain applies general standards of justification to testimony and public information; scientific experimental design has its own domain.

#### Knowledge and Justification

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Belief, truth, evidence, and knowledge have distinct roles in epistemic evaluation. Competing theories of knowledge can disagree about exactly how those roles connect.

##### Distinguish belief from knowledge

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize that sincerely accepting a proposition does not by itself establish that it is known, even when the speaker is confident.

##### Distinguish truth from justification

[Not implemented](#)

Explain why a claim might be true for reasons its holder cannot supply, or well supported yet ultimately false.

##### Evaluate reasons for a belief

[Not implemented](#)

Determine whether offered grounds actually bear on the proposition and withstand plausible objections.

##### Compare a priori and empirical justification

[Not implemented](#)

Distinguish support based primarily on reasoning from support requiring experience, without assuming the boundary is universally uncontested.

#### Evidence and Testimony

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Knowledge often depends on information supplied by other people or institutions. Source reliability, independence, and evidential fit require appraisal without treating every source as equally trustworthy.

##### Distinguish a claim from its evidence

[Not implemented](#)

Separate what someone asserts from the observations, documents, or arguments presented to support it.

##### Evaluate testimonial credibility

[Not implemented](#)

Consider the speaker's opportunity to know, relevant competence, track record, and possible conflicts without reducing evaluation to popularity.

##### Check corroboration across sources

[Not implemented](#)

Determine whether apparently agreeing accounts are genuinely independent or reproduce one underlying report.

##### Distinguish direct evidence from hearsay

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize what a record or witness directly establishes and what depends on another person's retelling.

#### Uncertainty and Skepticism

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Rational inquiry must accommodate incomplete evidence and the possibility of error without collapsing into indiscriminate doubt.

##### Calibrate confidence to evidence

[Not implemented](#)

Express degrees of certainty proportionate to the support available and identify what new information could change them.

##### Recognize the limits of personal knowledge

[Not implemented](#)

Differentiate what one has observed, inferred, learned from others, or cannot currently establish.

##### Evaluate a skeptical challenge

[Not implemented](#)

Assess whether a proposed possibility of error undermines a specific knowledge claim or sets an unrealistically absolute standard.

##### Identify a disagreement about evidence standards

[Not implemented](#)

Distinguish people contesting a factual premise from people applying different thresholds for accepting the same evidence.

#### Media and Public Information

Skills: 5

[Not implemented](#)

Information environments combine reporting, interpretation, promotion, and falsehood. Epistemic evaluation concerns the warrant for public claims, not the psychological mechanics by which messages persuade audiences.

##### Distinguish reporting from opinion

[Not implemented](#)

Identify whether a passage presents checkable occurrences, interpretation, advocacy, or a mixture requiring separate evaluation.

##### Trace a public claim to its original source

[Not implemented](#)

Follow citations or reposts to determine what a primary document, recording, or dataset actually says.

##### Evaluate evidence behind a headline

[Not implemented](#)

Compare a summary with the underlying article or evidence and identify claims that exceed what is documented.

##### Recognize misleading context in presented evidence

[Not implemented](#)

Detect how cropping, omitted baselines, selective examples, or missing dates can alter the apparent meaning of otherwise genuine material.

##### Distinguish misinformation from deliberate deception claims

[Not implemented](#)

Identify inaccurate information without asserting an author's intention to deceive unless independent evidence supports that inference.

### Philosophy of Science

Groups: 4 · Skills: 23

[Not implemented](#)

Scientific inquiry is examined as a way of building, testing, and revising explanations. The focus is on general methodology and evidential justification; physics, biology, psychology, and other sciences own their domain-specific investigations.

#### Questions and Hypotheses

Skills: 5

[Not implemented](#)

Research design starts with propositions that can be addressed using evidence. Questions, hypotheses, predictions, and observation serve different epistemic roles.

##### Formulate an empirically investigable question

[Not implemented](#)

Identify observations or comparisons that could bear on a precise research question, while recognizing questions outside empirical testing.

##### Formulate a testable hypothesis

[Not implemented](#)

State a possible answer whose empirical implications can be investigated rather than relying on an unfalsifiable restatement.

##### Derive a prediction from a hypothesis

[Not implemented](#)

Explain what should be observed under specified conditions if a proposed explanation is correct.

##### Distinguish observation from interpretation

[Not implemented](#)

Separate a recorded outcome from the theory-dependent explanation assigned to that outcome.

##### Compare observational and experimental research

[Not implemented](#)

Distinguish observing variation as it occurs from deliberately manipulating an explanatory factor.

#### Experimental Design

Skills: 7

[Not implemented](#)

Controlled investigations attempt to isolate explanatory factors while managing confounding, expectations, and ethical constraints. Particular safeguards solve different problems and are not interchangeable.

##### Identify independent and dependent variables

[Not implemented](#)

Distinguish the putative influence under study from the outcome measured in a proposed investigation.

##### Control relevant experimental conditions

[Not implemented](#)

Hold relevant background conditions stable or account for them so comparisons are not undermined by extraneous differences.

##### Explain the purpose of a control group

[Not implemented](#)

Identify the comparison condition needed to interpret an intervention's effect where a control group is appropriate.

##### Explain random assignment

[Not implemented](#)

Show how allocating participants or units by chance can reduce systematic pre-existing differences between comparison groups.

##### Explain single blinding

[Not implemented](#)

Identify which party is kept unaware of assigned conditions and how that may reduce particular expectation-related effects.

##### Explain double blinding

[Not implemented](#)

Identify when both participants and interacting researchers or outcome assessors are kept unaware of allocation, and why such a design is neither always feasible nor universally required.

##### Explain a placebo control

[Not implemented](#)

Distinguish a comparison intended to account for expectations or contextual effects from an active treatment or mere absence of intervention.

#### Evidence and Causal Inference

Skills: 5

[Not implemented](#)

Observations become informative only through assumptions about measurement, variation, and alternative explanations. The quality of a causal conclusion is not identical to the size of an observed association.

##### Distinguish correlation from causation

[Not implemented](#)

Explain why two variables varying together does not alone show that changing one produces the other.

##### Identify a confounding factor

[Not implemented](#)

Find a plausible third variable or selection process capable of producing an apparent relationship.

##### Assess sample representativeness

[Not implemented](#)

Examine whether selection and coverage allow findings to extend to the population named in a conclusion.

##### Assess measurement validity

[Not implemented](#)

Determine whether an operational measure genuinely captures the construct a researcher claims to study.

##### Evaluate a causal conclusion from a study

[Not implemented](#)

Judge how design, alternative explanations, limitations, and observed evidence jointly support or weaken a causal claim.

#### Theories and Scientific Practice

Skills: 6

[Not implemented](#)

Scientific conclusions are provisional but not arbitrary: shared procedures, criticism, reproducibility, and explanatory success distinguish responsible inquiry from mere assertion.

##### Distinguish a scientific theory from a hypothesis

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize a theory as a broad explanatory framework supported and refined through inquiry, not merely an untested guess.

##### Evaluate the falsifiability of a claim

[Not implemented](#)

Assess whether plausible observations could count against a proposition without treating falsifiability as the sole criterion of science.

##### Explain the role of replication

[Not implemented](#)

Show why repeating an investigation can expose chance results, context dependence, or methodological errors.

##### Evaluate scientific peer review

[Not implemented](#)

Distinguish expert scrutiny of methods and interpretation from a guarantee that published claims are correct.

##### Identify conflicts of interest in research

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize relationships that may create incentives to bias design, interpretation, or publication without assuming misconduct.

##### Explain scientific self-correction

[Not implemented](#)

Describe how independent criticism and new evidence can revise established models, without equating uncertainty with lack of knowledge.

### Metaphysics

Groups: 4 · Skills: 15

[Not implemented](#)

Metaphysics examines the most general categories of reality, such as what exists, how things persist, and what makes events possible. Its conceptual questions differ from describing particular natural mechanisms in the sciences.

#### Existence and Categories

Skills: 3

[Not implemented](#)

Different theories posit concrete objects, properties, events, or abstract entities; their classification determines what kinds of ontological commitments a claim makes.

##### Distinguish concrete from abstract entities

[Not implemented](#)

Assess whether a proposed entity has spatial or causal features characteristic of concrete things or is treated as abstract, such as a mathematical object.

##### Evaluate an existence claim

[Not implemented](#)

Examine the criteria by which something is said to exist rather than assuming that naming it settles its ontological status.

##### Distinguish properties from objects

[Not implemented](#)

Separate a bearer from qualities attributed to it, while recognizing that some metaphysical theories analyze this distinction differently.

#### Identity and Persistence

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Questions about sameness arise when material, properties, or relations change over time. Numerical identity is different from merely sharing characteristics.

##### Distinguish numerical from qualitative identity

[Not implemented](#)

Identify the difference between being one and the same entity and being indistinguishable in specified properties.

##### Evaluate persistence through change

[Not implemented](#)

Assess competing criteria for when an object remains the same despite replacement, growth, or alteration.

##### Analyze a part–whole relation

[Not implemented](#)

Examine whether a component and a composite have different identities and what changes follow from replacing parts.

##### Analyze a metaphysical paradox

[Not implemented](#)

Identify apparently plausible assumptions that generate inconsistent conclusions in a puzzle about reality.

#### Causation, Time, and Possibility

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Metaphysical inquiry asks what causal dependence means and how temporal or modal claims relate beyond specific measurements.

##### Distinguish causation from temporal succession

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize that one event occurring before another does not itself establish a productive or counterfactual dependence.

##### Compare philosophical accounts of time

[Not implemented](#)

Explain differences between views that privilege the present and views treating past, present, and future as equally real.

##### Distinguish possibility from necessity

[Not implemented](#)

Identify claims that could be otherwise and those held to be true in every relevant possible circumstance.

##### Evaluate a counterfactual claim

[Not implemented](#)

Examine what would follow if specified conditions had differed while keeping relevant background assumptions coherent.

#### Ultimate Reality and Religion

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Foundational arguments about transcendence, divinity, and ultimate explanation can be evaluated philosophically without endorsing or refuting any faith tradition as a curriculum requirement.

##### Reconstruct a philosophical argument about divinity

[Not implemented](#)

Identify premises and conclusion in a theistic, atheistic, or agnostic argument without treating the position as settled by its name.

##### Evaluate a proposed ultimate explanation

[Not implemented](#)

Consider whether an account of why anything exists explains more than it assumes and what further questions it raises.

##### Distinguish philosophical inquiry from religious practice

[Not implemented](#)

Separate reasoning about religious claims from performing rituals, accepting revelation, or participating in a tradition.

##### Analyze religious pluralism as a philosophical problem

[Not implemented](#)

Compare accounts of how conflicting ultimate-belief claims might be understood and evaluated while respecting freedom of conscience.

### Philosophy of Mind and Personhood

Groups: 4 · Skills: 17

[Not implemented](#)

Mental experience, personal continuity, and purposeful action raise conceptual questions not exhausted by neuroscientific or psychological measurement. This domain examines what such phenomena mean and their normative implications.

#### Mind and Consciousness

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

A first-person experience and a third-person physical description offer different perspectives on mental life. Philosophical theories disagree about how these perspectives relate.

##### Distinguish mental from physical descriptions

[Not implemented](#)

Identify when a claim describes subjective experience, observable behavior, or bodily processes, and why they are not automatically equivalent.

##### Compare theories of the mind–body relation

[Not implemented](#)

Contrast representative views such as physicalism and dualism by their commitments and explanatory challenges.

##### Explain the problem of other minds

[Not implemented](#)

Describe why another being's subjective experiences are not observed in the same way as one's own, and what kinds of inferences are used.

##### Analyze a philosophical problem of artificial consciousness

[Not implemented](#)

Assess what would and would not count as evidence of experience in an artificial system, without claiming a settled test exists.

#### Personal Identity

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

The self may be characterized by bodily, psychological, narrative, or relational continuity. The question is what makes someone the same person, not how memory works empirically.

##### Distinguish personal identity from similarity

[Not implemented](#)

Explain why sharing a personality or appearance is not enough to establish that two persons are numerically identical.

##### Compare bodily and psychological continuity accounts

[Not implemented](#)

Assess how different criteria handle change, memory loss, and hypothetical transfer scenarios.

##### Evaluate the role of memory in personal identity

[Not implemented](#)

Examine the philosophical significance and limitations of remembered experience as a criterion of persistence.

##### Analyze a personhood criterion

[Not implemented](#)

Test a proposed condition of being a person against borderline cases without assigning anyone lesser real-world dignity.

#### Agency and Free Will

Skills: 5

[Not implemented](#)

Acting for reasons, selecting among alternatives, and being accountable are connected but separable philosophical ideas.

##### Distinguish an action from an event

[Not implemented](#)

Identify whether a description invokes an agent's intention or reason rather than only something that happened to a body.

##### Distinguish reasons for action from causes of behavior

[Not implemented](#)

Separate justifying considerations from causal explanations, recognizing that the two may be related.

##### Compare determinism and indeterminism

[Not implemented](#)

Explain the different claims about how events are determined and why randomness alone does not establish control.

##### Compare compatibilist and incompatibilist freedom

[Not implemented](#)

Examine whether meaningful free action is possible under determinism and how rival views define freedom.

##### Evaluate conditions of moral responsibility

[Not implemented](#)

Consider knowledge, control, intention, and coercion in judging whether blame or praise is warranted.

#### Human Existence and Meaning

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Existential reflection examines how people understand purpose, authenticity, finitude, and commitments, without prescribing a single life ideal or substituting for mental-health care.

##### Distinguish a life purpose from a passing preference

[Not implemented](#)

Examine how sustained commitments and values differ from momentary wants when evaluating an account of a meaningful life.

##### Compare philosophical accounts of meaning in life

[Not implemented](#)

Contrast views grounding meaning in relationships, achievement, self-authorship, or other sources without assuming universal agreement.

##### Analyze authenticity as a philosophical concept

[Not implemented](#)

Evaluate competing claims about acting from one's commitments, social expectations, and self-understanding.

##### Analyze the philosophical significance of mortality

[Not implemented](#)

Examine how finitude affects arguments about priorities and meaning rather than making predictions about an individual's emotional response.

### Ethics and Moral Reasoning

Groups: 4 · Skills: 19

[Not implemented](#)

Ethics studies what ought to be done, what makes actions right or wrong, and how moral reasons apply to individuals and institutions. It evaluates normative standards rather than describing the psychological development of moral feelings.

#### Moral Concepts

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Ethical claims use concepts such as duty, harm, intention, and virtue that cannot be reduced to personal approval.

##### Distinguish moral from non-moral judgments

[Not implemented](#)

Identify when a claim concerns obligations or rights rather than taste, etiquette, prudence, or mere legality.

##### Distinguish intentions from consequences

[Not implemented](#)

Explain why an action's aim and its actual effects may be evaluated separately in moral reasoning.

##### Analyze moral duty

[Not implemented](#)

Identify what it means to hold that a person is obligated to act even when doing so is inconvenient.

##### Distinguish moral responsibility from causal responsibility

[Not implemented](#)

Separate causing an outcome from being appropriately accountable for it under relevant knowledge and control conditions.

#### Ethical Frameworks

Skills: 5

[Not implemented](#)

Normative theories emphasize different criteria of moral assessment. A theory is not a universal formula that automatically resolves every difficult case.

##### Apply consequentialist reasoning

[Not implemented](#)

Evaluate an action by outcomes and the welfare or values its consequences affect, accounting for affected parties.

##### Apply duty-based reasoning

[Not implemented](#)

Assess whether an act respects a rule, obligation, or person's standing even when violating it might have beneficial effects.

##### Apply virtue-ethical reasoning

[Not implemented](#)

Consider the traits and practical judgment exemplified by an action rather than evaluating only isolated rules or outcomes.

##### Apply care-ethical reasoning

[Not implemented](#)

Evaluate obligations arising from dependence, relationships, vulnerability, and context without treating care as the absence of principles.

##### Compare ethical frameworks on one dilemma

[Not implemented](#)

Explain why distinct normative standards may support different recommendations even when the underlying facts are shared.

#### Moral Deliberation

Skills: 5

[Not implemented](#)

Difficult cases require identifying who is affected, which reasons conflict, and whether an apparent compromise respects relevant obligations.

##### Identify stakeholders in an ethical dilemma

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize persons or other morally relevant parties affected directly or indirectly by a proposed choice.

##### Identify conflicting moral obligations

[Not implemented](#)

Specify duties or values that cannot all be fully honored in a concrete case without assuming that all conflicts are irresolvable.

##### Justify a moral judgment

[Not implemented](#)

Connect a normative conclusion to stated values, principles, and case facts rather than mere personal preference.

##### Evaluate a moral counterargument

[Not implemented](#)

Test whether an objection exposes an overlooked harm, inconsistency, or relevant exception to a principle.

##### Assess proportionality in moral decisions

[Not implemented](#)

Consider whether a harmful burden or restriction is justified in relation to its aim and less harmful alternatives.

#### Applied Ethics

Skills: 5

[Not implemented](#)

Ethical principles face distinctive challenges when rights, vulnerable people, nonhuman life, and emerging technologies are involved.

##### Evaluate consent in an ethical scenario

[Not implemented](#)

Assess whether a decision is adequately informed, voluntary, and within the decision-maker's authority.

##### Evaluate privacy as an ethical value

[Not implemented](#)

Reason about informational control, trust, and harms of disclosure beyond compliance with a particular law.

##### Evaluate duties toward nonhuman animals

[Not implemented](#)

Compare grounds for considering animal welfare or moral status in choices that affect sentient beings.

##### Evaluate environmental obligations

[Not implemented](#)

Examine duties to ecosystems and future generations, including trade-offs between present and long-term interests.

##### Evaluate ethical implications of automated decisions

[Not implemented](#)

Assess responsibility, contestability, fairness, and potential harm when an algorithm influences consequential choices.

### Social and Political Philosophy

Groups: 4 · Skills: 16

[Not implemented](#)

Political and social philosophy examines the justification of collective rules, institutions, authority, rights, and distributions of benefits and burdens. It asks normative questions rather than describing the chronology of regimes or the mechanics of markets.

#### Justice and Equality

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Claims of fairness depend on what is distributed, who qualifies for consideration, and which differences are morally relevant.

##### Distinguish equality from equity

[Not implemented](#)

Compare giving identical treatment with responding to relevant differences in needs or disadvantage, without assuming one always settles justice.

##### Compare distributive principles

[Not implemented](#)

Evaluate allocation by need, merit, equality, entitlement, or sufficiency and identify conflicts between principles.

##### Analyze procedural fairness

[Not implemented](#)

Assess the transparency, consistency, and opportunity to contest a decision independent of who benefits from its outcome.

##### Evaluate a claim about discrimination

[Not implemented](#)

Examine whether a distinction in treatment is relevantly justified or imposes an unfair burden on a protected or vulnerable group.

#### Rights and Liberty

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

A right may protect a person's action, claim, or status and create obligations for others. Competing rights can require principled reconciliation.

##### Distinguish rights from privileges

[Not implemented](#)

Identify whether a claim concerns an entitlement owed to a person or an advantage conditional on discretionary permission.

##### Distinguish negative and positive liberty

[Not implemented](#)

Contrast freedom from interference with capacities or opportunities to act, while recognizing debates about their relationship.

##### Analyze a conflict between rights

[Not implemented](#)

Identify whose claims compete and evaluate justified limits without assuming one right automatically cancels all others.

##### Explain freedom of conscience

[Not implemented](#)

Assess why protecting personal belief and nonbelief may matter independently of agreement with any particular worldview.

#### Political Authority

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Institutions exercise collective power that requires justification. Political legitimacy, coercion, and obedience are distinct concepts.

##### Evaluate the legitimacy of political authority

[Not implemented](#)

Examine reasons offered to justify the right to make binding public decisions, not merely whether an institution controls force.

##### Compare social contract arguments

[Not implemented](#)

Explain how alternative accounts use consent, cooperation, security, or fairness to justify political obligations.

##### Evaluate the limits of state coercion

[Not implemented](#)

Assess competing grounds for restricting individual actions, such as preventing harm or protecting rights.

##### Analyze civil disobedience

[Not implemented](#)

Consider the moral conditions, aims, and limits of nonviolent principled lawbreaking without equating legality with morality.

#### Public Reason and Civic Life

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

People with incompatible values must still make shared decisions. Philosophical inquiry examines how disagreements can be handled under pluralism and institutional accountability.

##### Distinguish public reasons from private preferences

[Not implemented](#)

Identify justifications aimed at principles others can evaluate, rather than asserting that a personal desire must settle collective policy.

##### Analyze democratic legitimacy

[Not implemented](#)

Evaluate the roles of participation, representation, minority protections, and accountability in justifying collective decisions.

##### Assess an argument about the common good

[Not implemented](#)

Examine who benefits, who bears costs, and whether a proposed collective interest disregards legitimate dissent.

##### Evaluate competing views of political obligation

[Not implemented](#)

Consider when citizens may have moral reasons to obey, criticize, or resist laws irrespective of mere fear of punishment.

### Philosophy of Language

Groups: 3 · Skills: 12

[Not implemented](#)

Language is examined as a system for conveying meaning, referring to the world, performing actions, and shaping interpretation. Grammar instruction and literary composition remain primarily in Language and Literature.

#### Meaning and Reference

Skills: 4

[Not implemented](#)

Words and sentences can express meanings beyond identifying objects; context, concepts, and the distinction between expression and referent affect analysis.

##### Distinguish a word from its referent

[Not implemented](#)

Explain why a linguistic expression and the person, object, or kind it denotes are not identical.

##### Distinguish sense from reference

[Not implemented](#)

Analyze how different expressions can pick out the same entity while presenting it in different ways.

##### Identify semantic ambiguity

[Not implemented](#)

Recognize when a sentence admits multiple readings due to a word or syntactic structure rather than simply lacking evidence.

##### Analyze a definition's scope

[Not implemented](#)

Test whether a proposed meaning wrongly excludes ordinary cases or includes cases outside its intended use.

#### Communication and Interpretation
